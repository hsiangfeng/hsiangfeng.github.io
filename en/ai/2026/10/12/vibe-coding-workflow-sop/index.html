30 Days of Vibe Coding | Day 29: My Vibe Coding Workflow SOP

Intro

We're down to the second-to-last day, so let's look back over the whole development process, step by step!

Think of it as wrapping up the entire Vibe Coding series, so in the future you can just follow along without having to dig back through Day 1 to Day 28.

The six steps at a glance

Looking back over the earlier days, we've actually been repeating the same six steps all along:

  • Think it through
  • Lay the foundation
  • Plan first
  • Take small steps
  • Set up quality gates
  • Go live

Each stage has its own tools that fit it best, so I've put them in a table:

StepWhat you doWhat you use
1. Think it throughTalk the requirements into a spec and acceptance criteriaInterview-style prompts, SPEC.md
2. Lay the foundationWrite the shared understanding into filesgit, CLAUDE.md, DESIGN.md
3. Plan firstRead the plan before approving big changesPlan Mode, docs/plans
4. Take small stepsOne feature at a time: build, check, commitThe three parts of a prompt, /clear, the debugging trio
5. Set up quality gatesMake code quality automaticcode-reviewer, Hooks, tests, PRs, CodeRabbit
6. Go liveMerge into main and it deploys automaticallyGitHub Actions, GitHub Pages

Honestly, this is pretty much how real-world development works too.

Vibe Coding makes development really fast, but before you let the AI loose, every stage has a checkpoint: the spec catches the direction, Git catches mistakes, the plan catches the scope, checks and tests catch quality, and the deployment flow catches the launch. These checkpoints work like brakes. They're what let you drive fast with confidence.

One thing to keep in mind, though: these six steps aren't a straight line. They're a loop that keeps going. Every update after launch starts again from step one.

Six-step flowchart

Hmm? Sounds a lot like Scrum (agile)? Yep, conceptually it's pretty similar. I've just broken each step down further and adjusted it for the way AI development works.

Note

Agile is more of a philosophy (a set of values and principles), and Scrum is one framework for putting it into practice. These six steps are a process I put together myself, not something from any official docs.

Where did each step come from?

Next, of course, let's talk about where each step came from, so you can see this process wasn't pulled out of thin air. Every step exists because we fell into a hole first.

Step 1: Think it through

Think it through

If you just tell the AI "make this page look better, you decide," the AI is basically just making it up as it goes.

So you end up saying over and over: no, not that, change it again, change it again. And now you're burning Tokens and time for nothing. It's like telling an engineer you want a system built without ever explaining what it's supposed to do. They have no choice but to fill in the gaps themselves.

So the way you use AI actually says a lot about how you usually communicate requirements to other people.

Step 2: Lay the foundation

Lay the foundation

Once the requirements are talked through, it's time to lay the foundation. Here, the foundation is:

  • Git: lets you go back to an earlier save point.
  • CLAUDE.md: write down the principles you and the AI agreed on, so it reads them in every new conversation.
  • DESIGN.md: write down what the screens look like, so it reads that in every new conversation.
  • SPEC.md: write down the requirements, so it reads them in every new conversation.

One thing to watch out for, though: when Claude Code starts a new conversation, it only reads CLAUDE.md automatically. So DESIGN.md and SPEC.md have to be pulled in from CLAUDE.md with @ (for example, a line that says @SPEC.md) for the AI to read them too.

This is also the stage where you settle on the tech you'll use. Don't just go with whatever tech the AI suggests. Ask it about the pros and cons first, and then you make the call.

Step 3: Plan first

Plan first

So next, do we just start building straight from the requirements in SPEC.md?

Nope. Don't forget the AI has its limits. Especially with big changes, it might miss some details or build something you didn't want.

So you should first have it write up a plan that lists which files it'll touch, what it won't do, and what the acceptance criteria are, and then you review it.

And have it save these Plan Mode plans in docs/plans/. Later, when a bug shows up, you can pull them out and check whether it actually followed the plan, or even use them to prove yourself wrong you're the one who said to do it this way, and then you blamed the AI for getting it wrong.

Step 4: Take small steps

Take small steps

Once the plan checks out, you can start building, of course. But here I'd recommend doing just one feature at a time.

Why? Say you eat three things you've never tried before and break out in hives. You'd have no idea which one caused it. It's the same with AI: if you ask it to change N features at once, when something breaks, you have no idea which change broke it.

So each round goes like this:

  • /clear first to wipe the last round's conversation and get a clean desk.
  • Ask the AI to build just one feature.
  • Check it yourself by hand, and only commit once it passes.

When you're checking, pay special attention to this: passing your checks doesn't mean there are no bugs. It might just mean you didn't happen to test that scenario. So put a few risky cases into your acceptance criteria on purpose, like data that crosses dates, last month's data, or an amount that exactly equals the budget. And click around the old features while you're at it, to make sure this change didn't break them.

If something really does break, don't worry. But never just say "it's broken, fix it" and send the AI off to change things. That's like going to the doctor and only saying "I don't feel well." The doctor can only guess. You have to describe clearly what's broken:

  1. How to reproduce it
  2. What you expected to see
  3. What you actually saw

That's basically a fixed debugging SOP.

If a few rounds back and forth still don't fix it, try asking from a different angle or narrowing down the scope. If that still doesn't work, use git to restore the broken files, go back to the last save point, and start over.

Step 5: Set up quality gates

Set up quality gates

The feature's done, so what keeps the quality up? Are you supposed to check everything yourself?

No way. People forget things, let alone AI. If you tell a coworker "remember to turn off the AC" every day before leaving, they'll remember most of the time, but there'll always be that one day they forget. A Hook is like setting the AC on a shutoff timer: when the time comes, it turns off, guaranteed.

So for things you need to check every time, like formatting and tests, don't just write them in CLAUDE.md and beg the AI to remember. Hand them to a Hook, so they run automatically the moment the time comes.

That doesn't mean you can skip checking things yourself, though. You still need to click through every new feature by hand once. It's just that the checks you'd repeat every single time get handed over to a mechanism. After all, with Vibe Coding you're not really reading the AI's code line by line, so something needs to keep an eye on the parts you don't see.

I split the gates into these:

  • Hooks: things that have to happen every time, like auto-formatting after the AI edits a file, or running the tests automatically after each round.
  • Tests: let the code check itself. Turn the acceptance criteria in SPEC.md into tests, especially for things like money calculations, where getting it wrong causes real trouble.
  • code-reviewer: a look-but-don't-touch subagent that reviews the AI's code with a fresh pair of eyes.
  • PRs: once you open a PR, GitHub Actions (GitHub's service that runs commands for you automatically) runs the tests and build, and CodeRabbit (an AI service that does code review for you automatically) reviews it one more time.

Pay special attention here: code-reviewer is a bit different from the other gates. It doesn't run on its own. It only goes to work when you call it. And sometimes the main agent decides it can just do the review itself (stubborn), and never hands it off at all. So after a feature is done, it's best to call it out directly with @, which guarantees it actually gets the job.

So why open a PR instead of pushing straight to main? Because every push to main deploys automatically, which means it goes live for everyone to see right away. So normally you branch off develop to do the work, then open a PR asking to merge back into develop when you're done. Think of a PR as a request form: you only hit merge after all the gates have run and you've looked at the results.

Also, just because a mechanism is set up doesn't mean it's running the way you think.

It's like installing a smoke detector at home but never pressing the test button, and then only finding out the battery died ages ago when there's an actual fire.

Take our formatting Hook. The files looked perfectly tidy, but it turned out later that Prettier had no config file at all. It had been formatting with its own defaults the whole time, so the quotes and semicolons didn't match the way the project was originally written. And since this kind of problem doesn't break any features, nobody noticed.

So after you set up a gate, remember to go back once and check that it's actually following the rules you want. Same goes for tests and GitHub Actions: break the code on purpose once and see whether it goes red, then change it back once you've confirmed it catches the problem. After all, if you've never seen it catch an error with your own eyes, how do you know it hasn't just been waving everything through?

And when it really does go red, don't panic. Don't merge yet. Paste the error message to the AI, have it fix things, then push to the same branch and let it run again.

As for the reports from code-reviewer and CodeRabbit, you do need to read them, but you don't have to fix everything. Handle the more serious ones first (like what CodeRabbit marks as high or medium), and talk the low ones over with the AI to see whether they really need fixing. The severity the AI assigns is just its opinion, not a royal decree. Whether to change something is still your call.

Step 6: Go live

Go live

All the gates have passed, so you can finally go live. But all-green Actions doesn't mean your site is fine.

It's like a food delivery app saying "Delivered." That doesn't mean the food is actually in your hands. Maybe it got left on the wrong floor. You still have to open the door and look.

What I mean is, all-green Actions only tells you the deployment process finished. For example, when you deploy to GitHub Pages, the URL gets an extra layer with the project name (like /money-note/). If vite.config.js doesn't set base, you'll open it to a blank white screen, and Actions will still be all green. So it only counts once you've opened the live URL and clicked around yourself.

Also, once you're on the PR workflow, the moment you merge develop into main is the moment you go live. So all those gates have to pass before you hit merge.

If it's your first time making a project public, then before you push, have the AI check the commit history for keys, passwords, .env files, and anything else that shouldn't be public. Once it's pushed, even if you delete the file afterward, it's still visible in the commit history.

And then back to step one

After launch, whether you're building the next feature or changing an old one, you start again from step one. And remember one big rule:

Update the rule docs first, then start building.

Why? Because in every new conversation, the AI works off docs like SPEC.md and CLAUDE.md. It's like a contractor building from blueprints: if you want to change the floor plan but don't update the blueprints, of course they'll keep building the old version.

Take our budget alerts. When we deliberately stuffed them into the plan early on, the "Out" list in SPEC.md blocked them. Later, when we actually wanted to build the feature, we went back to step one and updated the spec first, and that's how it got built smoothly.

As for what to build in the next loop, you don't have to come up with it out of nowhere. The docs/TODO.md you saved while taking stock of tech debt is a ready-made list of candidates. Just pick the smallest, lowest-risk one and start there.

Note

When you run into new holes in the future, please write them into the process too, so the process remembers what it cost you instead of relying on your own memory. As for where to write them, use the split we covered earlier: what to build goes in SPEC.md, what the screens look like goes in DESIGN.md, principles that take judgment go in CLAUDE.md, things that must happen every time go to a Hook, anything you've said three or more times becomes a Skill, and logic that causes trouble when it's wrong gets tests.

Slim the steps down, but don't skip them

At this point you might be thinking: going through all six steps just to change a button's color? That's exhausting.

Actually, how much each step involves can scale with the size of the feature. For a small feature like budget alerts, the interview only took around eight questions to get aligned, and the plan got approved as soon as the scope looked reasonable. The official best practices say something similar: if you can describe the change in one sentence, you don't need to plan first.

So how big counts as a big change? My own rule: if it'll touch two or more files, it's a project I'm not familiar with, or I don't really have a clear idea myself, I turn on Plan Mode first.

But slimming down and skipping are two different things. It's like heading out the door: you don't drag a suitcase along to grab breakfast at the corner, but you take your keys and phone no matter where you go. Acceptance criteria are those keys. No feature is too small for them, because the step you skip thinking you'll get away with it is usually the exact step that blows up later (don't ask me how I know).

SOP checklist for your next project

Here I've boiled the six steps down into one checklist. On your next project, go through it line by line for every feature you build:

  • Think it through: Can you say what you should see when it's done? Does SPEC.md cover what you'll build, what you won't, and the acceptance criteria?
  • Lay the foundation: Is git clean before you start? Are the decisions you agreed on all written into SPEC.md, CLAUDE.md, or DESIGN.md?
  • Plan first: Will it touch two or more files, is it an unfamiliar project, or do you not really have a clear idea yet? If so, read the plan first: which files it touches, what it won't do, and how it'll be checked.
  • Take small steps: Is this round just one feature? Did you check it by hand, including the risky cases and the old features, before committing?
  • Quality gates: Is npm test green? Did you call code-reviewer with @ to review it? Did you branch off develop, open a PR, and look at the Actions and CodeRabbit results?
  • Go live: After merging into main, did you open the live URL and click through it yourself?

Yep, that's it. Just these six.

Wrapping up

We're about at the end, so here's a quick summary:

  • My Vibe Coding workflow is: think it through, lay the foundation, plan first, take small steps, set up quality gates, go live, and then back to step one.
  • Every step exists because we fell into a hole first. When you hit new ones, write them into the process.
  • You can slim the steps down, but don't skip them. Acceptance criteria are never optional.
  • Processes you keep repeating can be turned into Skills and handed to the AI, but checking the work and deciding whether to merge and go live are still up to you.
  • Keep the resource list handy, and when you hit a technical question, always check the official docs first.
